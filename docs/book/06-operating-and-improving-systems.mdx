Shipping is the midpoint, not the finish line. Operations is how you learn
what your software actually does under real load - and how that learning
flows back into the next change.

## By the end of this chapter

You will know what to observe, how to run an incident calmly, and how to
turn every incident into a permanent improvement.

## Observe the four signals

Instrument these from day one:

| Signal | Question it answers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Latency | How long do requests take? | p50/p95/p99 response time |
| Traffic | How much is being asked of the system? | requests per minute |
| Errors | How much is failing? | 5xx rate, failed jobs |
| Saturation | How close to the limit are we? | CPU, memory, queue depth |

Alert on **symptoms users feel** (error rate, latency), not on causes
(high CPU). Pages that fire on causes create noise; pages that fire on
symptoms create urgency.

:::tip

Every alert must link to a runbook - the checklist an on-call engineer
follows at 3 a.m. An alert without a runbook is a notification, not
support.
:::

## Run incidents calmly

1. **Acknowledge and assign.** One incident lead; their job is
   coordination, not typing.
2. **Mitigate first, diagnose second.** Roll back, fail over, shed load -
   restore service before finding the root cause. (This is why the
   pipeline keeps artifacts per commit - see
   [Continuous Integration and Delivery](/docs/book/continuous-integration-and-delivery).)
3. **Communicate on a schedule.** A short update every 30 minutes, even
   "still investigating", beats silence.
4. **Write down the timeline as it happens.** Timestamped actions are the
   raw material of the postmortem.

## Postmortems without blame

A blameless postmortem studies the conditions that made the mistake
reasonable:

- Timeline: what was observed, decided, and changed, with timestamps.
- Contributing factors: code, config, process, capacity, assumptions.
- Action items: each with an owner and a date, prioritized by how much
  they reduce likelihood or impact.

Fix the system, not the person: "the deploy skipped canary because no one
owned that gate" leads to a gate; "Alice should have been more careful"
leads to the same incident with a different name.

## Improve on a rhythm

- **After every incident** - postmortem action items, tracked to
  completion.
- **Every sprint** - a small reliability budget: delete a noisy alert,
  tighten a timeout, add one missing metric.
- **Every quarter** - review the four signals from this chapter as
  trends; pick the one that is worst and make it the next quarter's
  focus.

## Close the loop

Operations produces the feedback that development needs: the alert that
fired reveals the missing test; the runbook step reveals the missing
script; the capacity wall reveals the next refactor. Feed it back through
the same channels the rest of the handbook uses - tests, pipeline, docs -
and the system gets a little more boring every release.

## Key takeaways

- Observe symptoms users feel; alert only where someone can act.
- Mitigate first, diagnose second, and communicate on a schedule.
- Blameless postmortems fix conditions, and their action items are
  tracked like any other work.
