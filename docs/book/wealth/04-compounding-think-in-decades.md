If you had invested $10,000 in Facebook stock on the day of its 2012 IPO
and held, the position would be worth many times that today - despite
crashes, scandals, and years of "Facebook is dead" coverage. The wealth
was not in the entry price. It was in the holding.

## By the end of this chapter

You will understand why compounding is back-loaded, know the three
behaviors that break it, and have a written policy for holding through
volatility.

## The shape of the curve

Compounding is counterintuitive because the gains hide themselves until
late. A 20% annual return on $10,000 looks like this:

| Year | Value | Gain that year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $12,000 | $2,000 |
| 5 | $24,900 | $4,980 |
| 10 | $61,900 | $12,400 |
| 20 | $383,400 | $76,600 |
| 30 | $2,373,800 | $474,800 |

The first five years produce about $15,000 of gain. The last five years
produce about $1.4 million. Most people quit during the quiet half of
the curve, right before the loud half begins.

:::tip

When your progress feels invisible, check the year table above. The
boredom is not a malfunction; it is the first twenty rows of the
spreadsheet.

:::

## Zuckerberg held; almost everyone else sold

The IPO collapsed within months of listing. The stock fell again in 2018
after the Cambridge Analytica scandal, and again in 2022 when Meta lost
over half its value. Through all of it, the founder's position stayed
essentially intact. He was not clever about the timing; he simply
refused to be forced out of his holding.

The retail investors who bought the same stock and sold during each
panic kept the losses and gave away the recovery. The difference in
outcome was not information - it was time horizon.

## Three behaviors that break compounding

1. **Selling in fear.** A drawdown is not a loss until you realize it.
   History says sharp drops are the admission price for high returns.
2. **Trading the noise.** Every switch between funds or stocks resets
   your clock, adds taxes and fees, and rarely beats simply holding.
3. **Spending the principal.** Cashing out the asset to fund lifestyle
   ends the curve immediately. Live on returns, not on the stake.

## Write a holding policy before you need it

Emotions are worst exactly when decisions matter most. The fix is a
policy written on a calm day:

- I do not sell positions because of news, predictions, or a single
  bad quarter.
- I rebalance on a fixed calendar, not on a fixed price.
- I may sell only for a reason that was on my written plan: a genuine
  change in the asset itself, or a planned purchase of another real
  asset.
- If I feel urgent pressure to sell, I wait thirty days first.

:::caution

Leverage is the one thing that can force you to break your own holding
policy. Margin loans and borrowed money let the market choose your exit
date for you. If you want to compound for decades, stay unforced.

:::

## Compounding beyond money

The same curve applies to everything Zuckerberg optimized for:

- **Skills.** A year of deliberate practice is invisible; a decade is a
  career-defining edge.
- **Reputation.** Every kept promise makes the next one worth more.
- **Audience and network effects.** Each user, reader, or contact makes
  the whole thing more valuable - for the network and for you.
- **Knowledge.** Concepts compound: what you learn today makes tomorrow
  cheaper to learn.

Choose activities where the value of the asset grows with its own
history, not activities where you start from zero every day.

## A decade-scale checklist

- [ ] Automate contributions so the curve runs even when motivation
      does not.
- [ ] Write the holding policy and store it where you will find it
      during a crash.
- [ ] Remove yourself from the decision: unfollow daily price feeds,
      mute hot takes.
- [ ] Judge the portfolio yearly, not monthly.
- [ ] Ask of every project: does year five build on year one, or start
      over?

## Start the clock early

Because the curve is back-loaded, the input that matters most is not
the return rate but the number of rows. Twenty years at 12% beats ten
years at 18%:

```text
$10,000 at 12% for 20 years = $96,500
$10,000 at 18% for 10 years = $52,300
```

A twenty-five-year-old contributing modestly holds an asset a
forty-year-old cannot buy at any contribution level. If you are
reading this late, the second-best time is now - the curve still
works, it just works on fewer rows.

Two practical consequences:

- Begin contributions today at whatever percentage is realistic;
  perfection is the enemy of the first row.
- Treat any windfall - bonus, tax refund, gift - as a time machine:
  it buys rows on the curve, not things.

## The boring middle is the whole game

Between the hopeful first years and the obvious last years sits a long
boring middle where nothing seems to happen. That middle is where the
curve is quietly loading. Show up for it on schedule, with automated
contributions and a written policy, and the loud half eventually
arrives - it always does for the people still holding. The decades
that look impressive from the outside were, from the inside, just the
same quiet routine repeated: contributions in, policy honored, noise
ignored, year after unremarkable year.

## Key takeaways

- Compounding is back-loaded: the quiet early years are normal, not
  failure.
- Zuckerberg's edge was not a better entry price; it was refusing to
  exit.
- Fear-selling, trading noise, and spending principal are the three
  curve-breakers.
- A written holding policy converts the hardest decision into a boring
  one.
- Skills, reputation, and knowledge compound on the same curve - pick
  games where history helps instead of resets.
