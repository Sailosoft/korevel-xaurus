Facebook was a small PHP application for years. Then it scaled to three
billion people without three billion additional hours of Zuckerberg's
labor. The gap between those two facts is leverage: work that gets done
once and pays out many times.

## By the end of this chapter

You will be able to classify the four kinds of leverage, pick one that
fits your skills today, and ship a first asset with a near-zero marginal
cost.

## The four forms of leverage

Naval Ravikant's framework is worth stealing whole:

| Leverage | Requires permission? | Marginal cost | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Labor (people working for you) | Yes | High | Hiring a team |
| Capital (money working for you) | Mostly yes | Low | Investing |
| Code | **No** | Near zero | A tool, a site, an automation |
| Media | **No** | Near zero | A video, a post, a course |

Code and media are the new leverage. They are the only kinds available
to someone with no employees, no investors, and no permission - which is
exactly the position Zuckerberg was in from a dorm room.

:::tip

Permissionless leverage is the reason a person with an ordinary job can
still build an extraordinary asset base. You do not need anyone's
approval to publish.

:::

## Why marginal cost matters

The economics are simple: if it costs you nothing to serve the next
customer, then every additional customer is nearly pure margin. A
consultant who bills hourly has a marginal cost equal to an hour of
their life. The same consultant who records a workshop once has a
marginal cost of a link.

Practical consequence: prefer work you can deliver as a **copy** over
work you must deliver as an **hour**.

## Building with code

You do not need to be a professional engineer. You need one small tool
that solves a real, boring problem.

Good starting shapes:

- A script that automates a task you already do weekly at work.
- A plugin or extension for a platform people already pay for.
- A micro-SaaS that charges $9/month to a narrow audience.
- A template repository or starter kit distributed for free, with a paid
  version.

Ship the smallest useful version this month. The point is not the
revenue in month one; it is that you now own an asset that exists,
rather than an idea that does not.

```text
Week 1  Pick one annoying, repeated task
Week 2  Build the ugliest version that works
Week 3  Give it to ten people and watch them use it
Week 4  Fix the top complaint, add a price, publish
```

## Building with media

Media is the second permissionless lever, and it compounds in a way most
people underestimate: a post written in 2019 still brings readers in
2026.

- Write about what you are already learning. Documentation is cheaper
  than creation.
- Publish on a schedule you can survive - monthly beats weekly that you
  abandon in March.
- Own the distribution. A newsletter list and an RSS feed are assets;
  a social following is rented land.

:::caution

Media leverage is slow at the front and steep at the back. The first
year usually feels pointless. That is the shape of the curve, not a
signal to stop.

:::

## Leverage on your current job

Even if you never launch a product, you can apply the principle inside
your employment:

- Automate the report nobody else wants to maintain, then own the
  system.
- Write the internal documentation that becomes the standard.
- Build the shared tool your whole team depends on.

That is reputation leverage: your name attached to a system that works
without you. It is how people become hard to replace and easy to
promote.

## Combining the levers

The strongest small assets stack them:

1. **Code** produces the tool.
2. **Media** produces the audience that finds the tool.
3. **Capital** - the revenue - is then reinvested into better tools and
   more reach.

This is the same loop, at a hundredth of the scale, that ran Facebook:
build the product, grow the network, reinvest the money, repeat.

## Counting the leverage you actually have

A useful audit: for one week, log every hour you work and mark each
one as either **consumed** (gone when the hour ends) or **deposited**
(adds to an asset that outlives the hour). Most people discover their
ratio is 95/5 consumed-to-deposited.

The shift does not require heroic effort - it requires re-sequencing:

- Do the deposited hour first, while attention is cheapest to give.
- Convert one consumed task per week into a deposited one: the report
  you write becomes the template others reuse.
- Track the ratio monthly. Moving from 5% to 20% deposited is enough
  to change your five-year outcome.

:::info

The leverage audit is the cheapest wealth tool in this book: a
notebook and one honest week. It tells you whether your effort is
building an asset or just producing motion.

:::

## Permissionless does not mean consequence-free

No one grants permission to publish, but the market still grants or
withholds attention. Leverage amplifies judgment, it does not replace
it: ship things you would be happy to explain in ten years, and let
quality be the filter that decides which of your assets survive.

## Key takeaways

- Code and media are permissionless leverage: no boss, no investor, no
  gatekeeper required.
- Prefer assets whose marginal cost of one more user is near zero.
- Ship one small thing per year; five years of that is a portfolio.
- Own your distribution - mailing lists and feeds, not just followers.
- Apply the same principle inside your job by automating and owning
  systems, not just tasks.
