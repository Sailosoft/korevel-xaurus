title: Ownership - Equity Over Salary
description: Chapter 2 - why Zuckerberg's fortune is a share count, not a paycheck, and how to convert your income into ownership.

Zuckerberg has never been paid much in cash. His salary was famously one
dollar. His wealth is a number of shares multiplied by a price. This
chapter is about the single most important structural decision in any
wealth plan: getting paid in ownership instead of only in time.

## By the end of this chapter

You will be able to describe the difference between earned income and
owned assets, know the four realistic routes to ownership on a normal
career path, and have a plan to negotiate or build your first equity
stake.

## The math of a salary versus a stake

A salary is a linear function: hours in, money out. It has a ceiling,
it is taxed at the highest rate you pay, and it stops the day you stop.

Equity is a nonlinear function: you own a slice of a system whose value
is decided by the market, not by your timesheet.

| | Salary | Equity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scales with | Hours worked | Value of the asset |
| Stops when | You quit or retire | Never (until you sell) |
| Tax treatment | Ordinary income | Capital gains, usually lower and deferred |
| Upside | A few percent per year | Multiples |
| Downside | Paycheck disappears | Value can go to zero |

:::tip

The goal is not to quit your job. The goal is to make sure that at
least one meaningful portion of your income each year converts into
something you own, rather than being fully consumed.

:::

## Route 1 - Equity compensation at work

If you work at a company that offers stock options, RSUs, or profit
sharing, that is the closest available copy of the Zuckerberg position.
Treat it as part of your compensation, not a bonus.

Before you sign anything, get clear on the mechanics:

- **Options vs. RSUs.** Options are the right to buy at a strike price;
  they are worthless if the value never crosses that strike. RSUs are
  actual shares that vest, and they are worth far more than candidates
  usually assume.
- **Vesting and cliffs.** Know the schedule, and know what happens on
  departure: is there an exercise window, or do you lose everything in
  90 days?
- **Dilution.** Your 0.5% becomes 0.3% after the next round. Ask what
  the fully diluted share count is.
- **Taxes.** In the US, an 83(b) election on early restricted stock can
  save enormous sums, but the window is 30 days and it is unforgiving.
  Read the rules before the deadline, not after.

The common failure mode is holding zero equity and then wondering why
high income produced modest net worth.

## Route 2 - Own a business, even a tiny one

You do not need a venture-backed company. A business you own 100% of is
a stronger equity position than 0.2% of a unicorn.

Small ownership looks like:

- A service business with one repeatable, documented process.
- A productized template, plugin, or digital good.
- A rental, a laundromat, a vending route - boring assets that produce
  cash flow.

What matters is that the asset exists separately from your hours. If
you can take two weeks off and the thing still earns, you have
ownership. If you cannot, you have a job with extra steps.

## Route 3 - Own the market through funds

Index funds are ownership too - you hold a claim on the earnings of
thousands of companies. This is the route with the lowest skill floor
and the highest reliability.

- Automate a fixed percentage of every paycheck into a low-cost total
  market index fund.
- Use tax-advantaged accounts first; the shelter is a real return.
- Never let the balance sit idle in cash waiting for a "better time".

:::caution

Equity in your employer concentrates your income and your savings in one
bet. If the company fails, you lose the job and the stake together.
Diversify out of single-employer stock as it vests.

:::

## Route 4 - Own your intellectual property

Royalties are ownership of an idea. Books, courses, music, software
licensing, and patents all pay you repeatedly for work done once. This
route is slower to start than a fund and faster than a business, and it
composes with both.

## How to ask for ownership

Most people never negotiate equity because they assume it is fixed. It
usually is not.

1. Ask early - in the first conversation, before an offer exists.
2. Ask in writing - get the grant number, strike price, vesting, and
   dilution assumptions in the offer letter.
3. Trade intelligently - a candidate who takes slightly less cash for a
   meaningful stake is buying an option on the company's upside.
4. If the answer is no, ask what would make it yes: a bonus tied to
   profit sharing, a phantom-equity plan, a deferred payout.

## Think like an owner, not a renter

The shift from salary to ownership is as much mental as financial.
Renters of their own career optimize for the next paycheck; owners
optimize for the value of the thing. Concretely:

- An owner asks "does this decision raise the asset's long-term
  value?" before asking "does this look good this quarter?"
- An owner maintains - skills, relationships, documentation - because
  maintenance protects future earning, not because it is visible now.
- An owner measures net worth, not income, as the scoreboard.

You can practice this before you hold a single share: run your
personal finances like a small company with a balance sheet. List
your assets (equity, funds, IP, property), your liabilities, and your
"revenue" streams. The page itself changes how you make decisions,
because suddenly the goal is not a bigger salary - it is a better
balance sheet.

:::info

The first time you receive a payment that did not require your hours -
a dividend, a royalty, a sale while you slept - something permanent
changes. You have felt ownership pay. Most people spend the rest of
their careers arranging their lives to feel it more often.

:::

## Key takeaways

- Zuckerberg's wealth is a **share count**; your wealth should stop
  being a paycheck count.
- Four routes to ownership: employer equity, your own business, index
  funds, and intellectual property.
- Learn the mechanics - vesting, dilution, and tax elections - before
  you sign, because the deadlines are short and unforgiving.
- Do not concentrate your income and your savings in the same company.
- Ask for equity. It is more negotiable than almost anyone expects.
