The previous nine chapters are individual levers. This one is the
assembly: how to put them into a single written plan you actually
execute, review, and revise for a decade. Zuckerberg's fortune was not
a series of clever moves; it was one coherent strategy held long enough
for every piece to compound at once. This chapter makes that possible
for you.

## By the end of this chapter

You will have a one-page wealth plan, a quarterly review ritual, a
five-year milestone map, and a clear-eyed answer to the question the
whole book exists to raise: what is the wealth for?

## Why written plans beat smart intentions

Most people's wealth strategy is a mood. "I should invest more" is not
a plan; it is a feeling that survives until the next purchase. The
founder habit that transfers is simple: write the strategy down, give
it dates, and review it on a calendar.

A written plan does three jobs:

1. **It decides in advance.** When a crash, an opportunity, or a
   temptation arrives, you consult the document instead of improvising
   while emotional.
2. **It makes drift visible.** Compared to last quarter's page, small
   betrayals of your own strategy show up immediately.
3. **It compounds identity.** Every completed review is evidence that
   you are the kind of person who executes. That self-image is worth
   more than any single tactic.

## The one-page plan

Fit the whole strategy on one page. If it does not fit, it is not a
plan, it is a wishlist.

```text
MY WEALTH PLAN - [year]

Main asset (Ch.2, Ch.3):
  One ownership vehicle I am building this year: ______

Contributions (Ch.6):
  __% of every paycheck, automated, day after payday

Safety layer (Ch.8):
  Emergency fund target: ______   Insurance gaps to close: ______

Focus (Ch.5):
  My one main thing: ______
  Three commitments I have said no to: ______

Experiments (Ch.7):
  This quarter's capped bet: ______ (cost cap $___, ___ hours)

Holding policy (Ch.4):
  I do not sell because of news. I rebalance on ___ and ___.

Definition of enough (Ch.10):
  The number and the date: ______
```

:::tip

Date the page and keep every old version. Watching your own plans
evolve is the most convincing proof of compounding you will ever see -
and the best defense against the feeling that nothing is happening.

:::

## The quarterly review ritual

Once every three months, two hours, same date each quarter:

1. **Numbers first.** Net worth, savings rate, contribution total,
   progress on the main asset. Write them down before reading anything
   else.
2. **Against the plan.** Which commitments held? Which quietly
   lapsed? No excuses - just facts.
3. **One adjustment.** Change exactly one thing per review. Plans that
   get rewritten monthly are plans being used as procrastination.
4. **Next quarter's experiment.** Pick the next capped bet (Ch.7) and
   write its kill metric before starting.

The review is where the flywheel closes: experiments inform the plan,
the plan directs the focus, the focus builds the asset, the asset
funds the next experiment.

## The five-year milestone map

Decade-scale thinking needs intermediate checkpoints or it collapses
into "someday". A sane map:

| Horizon | Milestone |
| --- | --- |
| Year 1 | Automated contributions running; emergency floor funded; first leverage asset shipped |
| Year 2 | Ownership stake meaningful (equity, business, or IP); holding policy tested by at least one real crash |
| Year 3 | Asset income visible - even $200/month changes your identity |
| Year 4-5 | Multiple ownership vehicles; the main thing is compounding without heroics |

If year five arrives and the map is empty, the diagnosis is almost
always one of two things: focus leaked (Ch.5) or the plan was never
written (this chapter).

## What is it for

The book's title is about being rich; the honest ending is about why.
Founder wealth is a means - autonomy, time, the ability to build things
that outlast you, the power to say no. Zuckerberg's post-2015 pivot
toward the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative is the classic arc: the fortune
became a tool for something beyond the scoreboard.

Ask the question explicitly, in writing, in your plan:

- At what number does more stop meaning freedom and start meaning
  administration?
- What will the asset buy you that money cannot buy later - time with
  people, health, the year to build the strange project?
- Who are you becoming in the process? A decade of focus shapes the
  person whether or not the net worth cooperates.

:::caution

The failure mode of this entire genre is arriving rich and late: the
plan executed perfectly while the reason got forgotten. Put the
"definition of enough" on the same page as the strategy, and review
both together.

:::

## Closing checklist

- [ ] One-page plan written, dated, and stored where you will see it.
- [ ] Contributions automated; lifestyle upgrades funded from returns
      only.
- [ ] Safety layers in place: cash floor, insurance, concentration
      limits.
- [ ] One main asset, one experiment per quarter, one review per
      quarter.
- [ ] Holding policy signed with yourself, like a treaty.
- [ ] "Enough" defined - so the long game has a direction, not just a
      speed.

## Key takeaways

- A written plan converts intentions into decisions you make calmly,
  in advance.
- Review quarterly, adjust one thing, keep every old version.
- Decade-scale goals need five-year checkpoints or they never arrive.
- The flywheel: experiments → plan → focus → asset → experiments.
- Define "enough" on the same page as the strategy; the means must
  never forget the end.

You will not become Mark Zuckerberg. The good news is that nobody had
to - the mechanics that made him rich are ownership, leverage,
patience, focus, reinvestment, capped risk, and a long game. They are
here in ten chapters, and they work at any starting line. The only
step left is the one he could not skip either: write the plan, start
the clock, and hold.
