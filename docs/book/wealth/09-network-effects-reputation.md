Facebook's moat was network effects: every new user made the product
more valuable for all existing users, which attracted more users, in a
loop that competitors could not break. The same loop runs on a human
scale. Your reputation and relationships are an asset with network
effects - and most people never invest in them deliberately.

## By the end of this chapter

You will see your network as a compounding asset, know the four habits
that grow it, and have a simple system for maintaining it without
becoming a networking robot.

## You are a platform too

The structural parallel is exact:

| Platform | Person |
| --- | --- |
| Users | Relationships and reputation |
| Value per user grows with network size | Opportunities compound: one referral leads to another |
| Lock-in through utility | Trust built by kept promises |
| Cold-start problem | The first introductions are the hardest |

Zuckerberg's earliest insight was that the value of a social network
grows with the square of its users. Your personal network behaves the
same way: the tenth connection who knows of you is worth more than the
first, because they know each other, refer each other, and pull you
into rooms you could not knock on.

:::tip

Reputation is the deferred payment system. Every visible, useful act
deposits into an account that pays out years later, usually as an
opportunity you did not have to ask for.

:::

## The four habits that compound

1. **Be reliably useful.** Not sycophantic, not transactional - just
   the person who does what they say and says what they do. Trust is
   the currency; usefulness is how you earn it.
2. **Publish your work.** A post, a talk, an open-source repo, a
   documented project. Silent competence has no network effects;
   visible competence attracts the right strangers while you sleep.
3. **Connect others generously.** The highest-leverage act in any
   network is an introduction between two people who both benefit. It
   costs minutes, and both parties remember you forever.
4. **Keep in touch on a schedule, not on need.** Reaching out only when
   you want something teaches people what you are. A brief quarterly
   note - "saw this, thought of your project" - keeps the graph warm.

## Choose rooms with increasing returns

Where you spend relationship time matters enormously:

- **Builders around builders.** Rooms where people ship things raise
  your baseline and generate collaboration.
- **Asymmetric mentors.** One hour with someone ten years ahead who
  has what you want is worth ten hours with peers. Approach them with
  specific work, not vague admiration - "I built X, stuck on Y" beats
  "can I pick your brain".
- **Your existing graph first.** The strongest opportunities usually
  come from weak ties - acquaintances of acquaintances - not from
  strangers. Your dormant contacts are an untapped asset; the quarterly
  note reactivates them.

:::caution

Avoid the collector fallacy: adding contacts is not building a network.
A graph of 2,000 strangers with zero trust behaves like a graph of
zero. Depth and reciprocity are what create the compounding; count is
vanity.

:::

## The dark side: networks amplify what you are

Network effects are indifferent to quality - they multiply your
reputation whatever it happens to be. One broken promise in a small
room travels. A reputation for cutting corners compounds at the same
rate as a reputation for excellence, and is far harder to unwind.

This is why the focus and reinvestment chapters matter here: the
fastest way to destroy a compounding reputation is to chase quick money
in ways that make you look clever in a quarter and unreliable over a
decade.

## A simple maintenance system

Do not overengineer this. A workable weekly/biweekly loop:

- [ ] Once a week: send one introduction or one genuine "saw this,
      thought of you" message.
- [ ] Once a month: publish one piece of visible work.
- [ ] Once a quarter: walk your list of ten important people and write
      to each briefly.
- [ ] Once a year: attend or host one gathering that mixes your
      different worlds - the collision is where new opportunities
      breed.

Track the asset the way you track money: not "how many people do I
know", but "who would answer if I called, and who would call me".

## Solving the cold start

Every network has the early problem Facebook had: it is worthless
before it is useful. The founder's trick was to make it valuable for
one small, dense group first - Harvard houses - before expanding.

The personal version:

- Pick one dense community: your team, your open-source project, your
  local builder group. Depth there beats breadth anywhere.
- Be the hub before you are the star: host the meetup, curate the
  thread, summarize the field. Utility attracts the graph.
- Expand along trust lines, not cold outreach: each new circle should
  arrive through an existing member.

A network that starts small and dense behaves like the platform
version: it becomes worth more than the sum of its edges, and it
becomes nearly impossible to copy.

## Give before the graph pays

Every compounding network runs on early deposits: help with no
prospect of return, introductions with no fee, credit given freely.
The account pays out later, unevenly, and usually from directions you
never aimed it at - which is exactly what "network effect" means.

## Key takeaways

- Your reputation and relationships follow the same math as a
  platform: value grows with the network, not linearly.
- Be reliably useful, publish work, connect others, and keep in touch
  on a schedule.
- Weak ties and dormant contacts are where outsized opportunities
  usually come from.
- Networks multiply whatever reputation you have - the compounding is
  morally indifferent.
- Maintain the graph with a tiny weekly habit, not with grand gestures.
