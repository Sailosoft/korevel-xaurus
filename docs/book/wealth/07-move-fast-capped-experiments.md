title: Move Fast - Experiments With Capped Downside
description: Chapter 7 - the "move fast and break things" habit translated into cheap, reversible experiments that generate information and asymmetric upside.
---

Facebook shipped constantly, broke things, and fixed them faster than
anyone else could. The strategy worked because the experiments were
cheap relative to the information they produced. This chapter turns that
into a personal operating procedure: run many small, reversible bets
where the downside is tuition and the upside is a new asset.

## By the end of this chapter

You will be able to tell reversible from irreversible decisions, design
an experiment with a capped downside, and run a repeating monthly
loop that leaves a written learning behind every time.

## Speed is an information strategy

Most people mistake "move fast" for recklessness. It is the opposite:
a plan is a guess wearing a costume. Reality only answers questions,
and the person who asks ten cheap questions this quarter knows more
than the person who perfects one plan for a year.

Zuckerberg launched the news feed before anyone knew if it would work.
He launched Reels years late but fast, and it became one of the
company's biggest defensive wins. The pattern: decide, ship, measure,
correct - with the correction budgeted in from the start.

:::tip

Be stubborn about the vision and flexible about the details. The
founders who fail invert this: they are flexible about the vision
(changing direction every month) and stubborn about the details
(polishing a feature nobody asked for).

:::

## Reversible versus irreversible decisions

Borrow Amazon's one-way/two-way door frame and apply it to your money
and career:

| | Two-way door (reversible) | One-way door (irreversible) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Examples | Launch a $50 side project, take a course, publish a post, test a price | Sign a mortgage, quit without runway, co-found a company, take on partners |
| Right speed | Fast - decide in a day | Slow - decide in weeks, with counsel |
| Cost of being wrong | Tuition | Years |

Almost every "startup decision" people agonize over is a two-way door.
They treat launching a website like signing a treaty. Speed comes from
correctly classifying the door.

## Cap the downside first

Before any experiment, write the loss limit. If the worst case is
survivable and bounded, the experiment is allowed to be fast.

- Budget it: "This project gets $300 and 40 hours. When either runs
  out, it stops or restarts as a new decision."
- Time-box it: "Thirty days, then I review the numbers and choose
  again."
- Isolate it: run experiments from a separate account or LLC so a
  failure cannot touch the core finances.
- Pre-name the kill metric: "If fewer than 10 strangers want this after
  30 days, I archive it without drama."

```text
Experiment template
  Hypothesis: I think [audience] will pay/use [thing] for [reason].
  Cost cap:   $___ and ___ hours
  Duration:   ___ days
  Kill metric: ___
  Learning log: written on day 1 and day N, no exceptions
```

:::caution

Capping the downside is what makes speed ethical toward your own life.
Never run a fast experiment with money you need for rent, never test a
business idea by quitting without runway, and never let an experiment
quietly become a commitment by forgetting to review it.

:::

## Failures are tuition - if you invoice for them

The difference between a failed experiment and wasted money is the
written lesson. After every loop, answer three questions in a notebook
that becomes your most valuable asset over time:

1. What did I believe going in?
2. What actually happened, in numbers?
3. What will I do differently, and what did this teach about where my
   real edge is?

Five years of this is a personal research archive most people cannot
buy at any price - it is a map of where you uniquely work.

## The monthly loop

Make experimentation a habit with a cadence you can keep alongside a
job:

- **Month 1:** ship the smallest version of one idea.
- **Month 2:** put it in front of strangers. Real users, not friends.
- **Month 3:** kill it or charge for it. Both are wins - either you
  freed 40 hours or you found revenue.
- Repeat. Twelve loops a year, two or three survivors every couple of
  years, is enough to change your net worth.

The compounding here is not just in surviving assets. Each loop makes
the next loop faster, cheaper, and better aimed. That is why the habit
matters more than any single project.

## Keep a decision log

Founders write postmortems; you can do the same at personal scale. A
decision log is one line per meaningful choice:

```text
2026-03-11 | Cancelled the gym I never used | saves $45/mo, reallocated
2026-04-02 | Said no to contract work       | protected launch window
2026-05-20 | Launched template at $19       | 4 sales in week one
```

Reviewing twelve months of these lines shows you, in your own data,
which kinds of bets actually work for you - and which excuses you
keep repeating. The log turns raw experience into evidence, and
evidence is what the next experiment should be built on.

## Borrow other people's speed

You do not have to discover everything by your own experiments. Study
people three years ahead of you on the same path: their failures are
free tuition, their checklists are pre-tested. One conversation with
someone who has already run your experiment can replace three months
of guessing - then run your version fast anyway, because borrowed
maps still need your own walking.

## Speed without the mess

Fast does not mean sloppy. Cap the scope, not the craft: ship a tiny
thing that is genuinely well made. One polished experiment teaches
more and builds more reputation than ten rushed ones, and reputation
is the multiplier on every future launch.

## Key takeaways

- Speed is an information strategy: reality answers questions, not
  plans.
- Classify the door first. Two-way doors deserve fast decisions.
- Cap the downside in money, time, and blast radius before starting.
- Write the lesson after every experiment, or the tuition is wasted.
- Run a repeating monthly loop; the habit compounds even when
  individual projects do not.
