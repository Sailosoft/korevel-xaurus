# Site Selection and Terrain

**Objective:** Explain criteria and methods for selecting stronghold sites, with emphasis on topography, hydrology, access, concealment, and urban-rural-elevated comparisons. This chapter is Chapter 2 of 10 in the Stronghold and Resilience series.

## Topographic Assessment

Topography determines observation, fields of fire, drainage, construction cost, and movement difficulty. Elevated positions provide extended sightlines and complicate approach by hostile groups. Depressions and valley floors facilitate water collection but increase flood exposure and restrict observation.

Assessment begins with contour analysis. Gradients above 15 percent impede vehicle movement and burden supply transport. Gradients between 3 and 8 percent favor drainage while permitting construction without extensive terracing. Flat ground simplifies building but requires artificial elevation for observation and deliberate drainage works.

Key topographic features for evaluation include:

- **High ground adjacency:** Proximity to dominating heights that permit observation into the site.
- **Slope aspect:** Orientation relative to sun, wind, and likely avenues of approach.
- **Micro-relief:** Small ridges, berms, and depressions that provide concealment or channel movement.
- **Soil stability:** Load-bearing capacity, erosion susceptibility, and excavation difficulty.
- **Rock exposure:** Availability of construction material versus impediment to digging and drainage.

Survey methods combine map study, aerial imagery, and ground reconnaissance. Map study identifies candidate areas. Imagery reveals vegetation patterns, erosion gullies, and access tracks. Ground reconnaissance verifies soil conditions, confirms sightlines, and detects seasonal hazards such as landslide scars.

## Hydrology and Water Security

Water availability governs site viability. A site without reliable water requires continuous importation, which contradicts autonomy objectives. Evaluation covers groundwater, surface water, precipitation capture, and drainage.

Groundwater assessment includes well depth, recharge rate, mineral content, and contamination risk from agricultural runoff, industrial discharge, or latrines. Shallow wells reduce pumping energy but exhibit greater vulnerability to drought and pollution. Deep aquifers offer stability at higher extraction cost.

Surface water provides supplementary supply and a defensive obstacle. Rivers and lakes support intake systems but attract attention and require purification. Seasonal streams cannot serve as primary sources.

Drainage analysis prevents structural damage and disease. Indicators of poor drainage include standing water after rainfall, hydrophilic vegetation, mottled soils, and foundation cracking in existing structures. Mitigation includes grading, French drains, culverts, and elevated floor levels. Sites on moderate slopes with permeable soils and defined runoff paths present the lowest drainage burden.

Water security planning specifies liters per person per day for drinking, cooking, hygiene, and medical use, plus reserves for fire suppression and decontamination. Storage capacity must bridge the longest plausible interruption of resupply.

## Access, Chokepoints, and Lines of Movement

Access analysis balances the need for supply and evacuation against the need to restrict unauthorized entry. Ideal configurations provide one or two controllable routes for occupants plus difficult terrain elsewhere.

Chokepoints such as bridges, passes, narrow roads, and gated entries permit monitoring with limited personnel. Each chokepoint requires an observation method, a barrier method, and a response procedure. Natural chokepoints formed by cliffs, water bodies, or dense vegetation reduce construction requirements.

Evaluation criteria include:

| Criterion | Favorable Condition | Unfavorable Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Approach routes | Single narrow road with visibility | Multiple open approaches |
| Adjacent cover | Open ground beyond 300 meters | Dense concealment near perimeter |
| Evacuation options | Secondary footpath plus vehicle route | Single route vulnerable to blockage |
| Supply proximity | Local sources within controlled area | Distant sources across uncontrolled ground |
| Traffic patterns | Low background movement, anomalies visible | High background movement, anomalies masked |

Road quality, bridge load limits, winter passability, and fuel requirements shape resupply feasibility. Helicopter landing zones or open fields extend options where ground routes remain fragile.

## Concealment, Exposure, and Signature Management

Concealment reduces the probability of detection and targeting. Vegetation, terrain masking, and integration with existing structures lower visual, thermal, acoustic, and electromagnetic signatures.

Visual concealment employs natural canopy, earth-toned materials, and avoidance of straight lines and reflective surfaces. Thermal concealment uses insulation, underground spaces, dispersed heat sources, and nighttime operational discipline. Acoustic concealment relies on distance, terrain barriers, and muffling of generators and machinery. Electromagnetic concealment limits radio emissions, employs directional antennas, and shields critical electronics.

Exposure decisions involve trade-offs. Complete concealment complicates resupply and communication. Visible deterrence discourages opportunistic intrusion but invites deliberate targeting. The appropriate balance depends on threat assessment. Low-threat environments favor discreet integration. High-threat environments favor hardened visibility combined with active defense measures described in Chapter 5.

## Urban Versus Rural Versus Elevated Contexts

Each context presents distinct advantages and constraints.

**Urban sites** offer proximity to medical care, materials, and labor, plus concealment within normal activity. Constraints include dependence on municipal utilities, limited space for stockpiles, proximity to large populations during unrest, and structural vulnerability to fire spread. Suitable urban strongholds occupy reinforced concrete or masonry buildings with independent water storage, backup power, controlled lobbies, and roof access for observation and rainwater capture.

**Rural sites** provide space, distance from population pressure, and potential for agriculture and wells. Constraints include extended supply lines, limited emergency support, and visibility of new construction in open landscapes. Suitable rural strongholds combine dispersed structures, tree lines for screening, and all-weather road access.

**Elevated or mountainous sites** deliver natural defense, gravity-fed water, and observation dominance. Constraints include harsh weather, landslide and avalanche risk, difficult construction logistics, and evacuation complexity. Suitable elevated sites occupy sheltered shoulders below ridgelines rather than exposed summits, preserving observation while reducing wind exposure.

Selection matrices score each candidate site across water, access, concealment, construction feasibility, and expansion potential. No site maximizes all factors. Decision records document accepted compromises and compensating measures.

## Site Selection Process and Documentation

A structured process prevents omission of critical factors. Recommended stages include definition of requirements, desktop screening of candidates, field verification, comparative scoring, and preparation planning.

Documentation includes coordinates, boundary descriptions, ownership and legal status, hazard maps, water test results, photographs of approaches, and infrastructure inventories. Maintenance of this dossier supports adaptation and recovery decisions covered in Chapter 9 and continuity planning covered in Chapter 6. Thorough selection reduces fortification cost, simplifies sustainment, and establishes the physical foundation for all subsequent resilience measures.
