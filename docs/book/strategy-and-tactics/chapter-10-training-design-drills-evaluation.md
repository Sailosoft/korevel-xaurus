# Chapter 10 — Training Design, Drills, and Evaluation

**Series:** Strategic Advance & Tactical Operations Trainer (Chapter 10 of 10)

---

## 10.1 The Training Philosophy

Train movements as they will be fought: under load, at tempo, with friction injected. The progression is fixed:

1. **Drill** — single action, repeated to reflex, no opposition.
2. **Problem** — combined actions, timed, against a static scenario.
3. **Exercise** — adaptive, thinking opposition, with communication degradation and casualties.
4. **Live integration** — full force, full logistics, real terrain, real night.

No unit advances to the next level until the previous one is automatic. Drills that require thinking in the exercise are not drills. The training management cycle that drives the progression has four steps: **analyze** the unit's mission and current standard; **plan** the training to close the gap; **execute** to standard with measured outcomes; **assess** and record, feeding the next analysis. A unit's training calendar is a curriculum, not a schedule of events.

## 10.2 The Core Drill Library

Every movement unit maintains reflex-level proficiency in:

| Drill | Trigger | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Halt / bound / resume | Call sign | Whole column responds without explanation |
| Near ambush reaction | Contact < 50 m | Assault through within 15 s |
| Far ambush reaction | Contact > 50 m | Suppress, flank, report sequence initiated |
| Choke-point halving | Bridge, defile, tunnel | Elements pass alternately, never all at once |
| IED/obstacle discovery | Suspicious item | Freeze, mark, report, covered bypass |
| Casualty movement | Wounded in column | Trail element recovers; column does not halt |
| Comms failure | Lost primary | PACE switch executed without halting movement |
| Night movement | Light order | Rate maintained with no external illumination |

Each drill is trained cold, then under stress (time limits, noise, role-player opposition), then in combination. The combination step is where most programs stop too early: drills that survive stress individually but collide when simultaneous have not been trained — they have merely been practiced.

## 10.3 Exercise Design

Design movement exercises around **decision points, not terrain walks**:

- Write the scenario as a series of triggers: obstacle at the bridge, radio silence window, friendly-fire risk, civilian crowd, logistics shortfall at hour 20.
- The opposition force (OPFOR) gets its own mission: delay, deceive, or template — never merely "be present."
- Injects must include at least one that invalidates the plan's central assumption; how the leader adapts is the actual assessment.
- Control the exercise with safety and fairness, but never rescue a unit from a consequence that the scenario can survive.

Exercise scale should match the training objective: squad-level exercises test drill execution, platoon and company tests coordination and handovers, and only battalion-and-above tests the tempo game of Chapter 7 — decision cycles, reserves, and logistics deadlines. A force that trains only at the level of its comfort never discovers the level at which it breaks.

## 10.4 Evaluation Standards

Grade movement on the five pillars, measurably:

- **Purpose:** did the movement achieve the stated effect? (binary)
- **Direction:** deviation from planned axis, in distance and time.
- **Tempo:** time from trigger to initiation; bounds per hour versus plan.
- **Protection:** security posture violations observed; surprise events suffered.
- **Sustainment:** fuel, ammunition, and readiness state on arrival.

Each pillar carries numeric evidence collected during execution — observer cards, clock times, state reports — so the after-action review argues from data, not memory. After-action review runs in three passes: what was intended, what happened, what changes for next time. The unit writes its own corrections; the trainer verifies they are executed in the next repetition. An AAR without a recorded correction and a verified repetition is theater.

## 10.5 The Trainer's Craft

The trainer is trained the same way the unit is. Standards for the cadre:

- **Demonstrate first:** every drill is shown at standard before it is demanded; the trainer who cannot execute the bound cannot grade the bound.
- **Inject deliberately:** friction is a teaching tool, applied on a schedule that peaks at the end of each training day, when cognition is thinnest and habits are tested.
- **Grade against standards, not effort:** the unit that tried hard and arrived late is late.
- **Protect the progression:** no unit runs the exercise until the drill is automatic; an exercise that fails because the drill failed wastes the scenario and teaches the wrong lesson.

## 10.6 Sustainment of Standards

Proficiency decays faster than it is built; the training program must defend what it has won:

- **Monthly requalification** on the core drill library, graded to the same standards as the original certification. A drill that is not re-verified is a drill that is being forgotten.
- **Randomized spot drills** during routine movement — an unannounced halt, bound, or comms-failure call — so readiness is a property of the unit, not of the training calendar.
- **Personnel turnover loops:** every replacement receives the drill library cold-to-stress within one cycle, and the unit's standard is re-proven after each significant intake, because a unit is only as reflexive as its newest member.
- **Standards drift audits:** trainers compare current execution against recorded baselines quarterly; the enemy of a trained force is not the first lapse but the accepted one.

The sustainment mindset treats excellence as maintenance, not achievement: the column that moves like one body must be fed that practice monthly, or it reverts, quietly and completely, to a collection of vehicles going somewhere.

## 10.7 Certification for the Operational Movement Trainer

A trainer is certified when they can:

1. Build a four-phase advance plan with bounds, decision points, and logistics computed to the limit.
2. Conduct and control all eight core drills to standard.
3. Design and run a two-day movement exercise with an adaptive OPFOR and a plan-invalidating inject.
4. Evaluate any movement problem against the five pillars with numeric evidence.
5. Teach the full content of Chapters 1–9 from memory, in field conditions, without slides.

## 10.8 Closing Doctrine

Strategy decides where the force must arrive. Tactics decide whether it arrives able to fight. Movement is the only activity that must satisfy both at once. Train it until the column moves like one body, halts like one decision, and fights like one intent — and the advance will outpace every enemy plan written against it.
