# Strategic Advance & Tactical Operations Trainer
## Chapter 4 — Tactical Maneuver Principles

**Series:** Strategic Advance & Tactical Operations Trainer (Chapter 4 of 10)

---

## 4.1 Maneuver Is About Position, Not Destruction

Maneuver places the force where the enemy cannot effectively respond. Destruction is the by-product; positional advantage is the aim. Three laws govern it:

1. **Always act against the enemy's decision cycle, not their order of battle.** Strike where their next plan does not expect you.
2. **Fix and flank.** A force that cannot fix the enemy in place cannot maneuver around them; the flank attack fails into a frontal fight.
3. **Concentrate decisively, disperse vulnerably.** Mass combat power at the point of decision; keep the rest of the force dispersed and survivable.

The moral mechanism of maneuver is disproportion: a small positional threat that forces the enemy to redeploy large forces achieves more than a large attack that costs the enemy equal forces. Every maneuver plan should therefore ask what the enemy must do in response, and whether that response costs them more than your action costs you.

## 4.2 The Five Tactical Schemes of Movement

- **Frontal attack:** pins by direct pressure. Use only as a supporting scheme — never the main effort unless the enemy is already broken.
- **Envelopment:** avoid the strong point, seize the objective from a flank or rear while a fixing force holds attention forward.
- **Turning movement:** bypass the force entirely, aiming deep at logistics and command. The enemy must abandon positions to save their base.
- **Infiltration:** movement in small elements through gaps, at night, without engaging. The precursor to all other schemes in contested areas.
- **Penetration:** rupture a prepared position at a narrow point and widen the gap for exploitation forces. Requires suppressive fires, breaching tools, and a committed reserve.

Each scheme demands a different movement anatomy. Envelopment requires a fixing force strong enough to be credible and a maneuver force fast enough to win the race to the flank. Turning movement requires logistics that can survive operating beyond friendly support. Infiltration requires discipline not to fight when tempted. Penetration requires the brutal arithmetic of breaching: suppress, breach, clear, exploit — in overlapping waves, never sequentially.

## 4.3 Supporting Attacks, Feints, and Demonstrations

- A **supporting attack** wins a secondary area to free forces or fires for the main effort.
- A **feint** seeks to deceive through actual engagement — it fights to mislead.
- A **demonstration** shows force without engaging — cheaper, but a weaker lie.

Deception of any kind only works if it confirms what the enemy already suspects. Design movements against enemy beliefs, not enemy facts. A supporting attack that the enemy reads as the main effort has succeeded twice over — but only if its strength is calibrated to be believable and its exit criterion is defined before it begins.

## 4.4 Economy of Force and the Reserve

Maneuver consumes forces at the point of decision, so the plan must economize everywhere else: the smallest force that can fix, screen, or hold. The classic error is the "rob Peter to pay Paul" economy that strips the reserve to reinforce a supporting attack — the reserve is the commander's ability to respond to the unexpected, and a plan that spends it before the decisive moment is a plan that has already been defeated by the first surprise.

Reserve employment rules: commit on pre-briefed trigger conditions, never on optimism; commit the minimum needed to win the crisis, keeping capacity for a second; and once committed, the plan must name the new reserve, even if it is "none, with consequences accepted."

## 4.5 Hasty versus Deliberate

Default to the **hasty attack** when the enemy is disorganized, the tempo advantage is perishable, or the mission requires speed. Shift to **deliberate** execution when facing prepared defenses, when force ratios are marginal, or when the cost of failure is unrecoverable. The trainer's rule: a hasty attempt buys information; a deliberate attempt buys a result. Decide which you are buying before you commit.

The decision is made once, by the commander, on the criteria set before movement. Leaders who switch between hasty and deliberate mid-engagement — reinforcing a hasty attack piecemeal — produce the worst outcome of both: attrition without surprise.

## 4.6 Pursuit and Retrograde as Maneuver

Maneuver does not end at the objective. **Pursuit** converts victory into defeat of the enemy force as a fighting entity: a direct force pins and fixes while a parallel force races for the line of escape. Pursuit succeeds on tempo and fails on logistics; the unit that cannot refuel cannot pursue, and the enemy's retreat becomes their reconstitution. **Retrograde** — withdrawal, delay, retirement — is maneuver executed away from the enemy, and is governed by the same laws: fix the enemy's attention, bound under covering fire, and disengage by displacement rather than by flight. The force that practices retrograde as a scheme learns that movement away from contact is still movement with intent.

## 4.7 Fires That Make Movement Possible

Maneuver is movement supported by fire; without fire, it is simply travel exposed. The supporting fires plan for a moving force answers four questions before departure:

- **Who suppresses the contact point** when the lead element fights, and from what position on the flank rather than behind?
- **Who displaces** when the enemy locates the firing position, and on what interval?
- **What is the lift and shift schedule** as the maneuver force closes on its objective — and what is the danger-close standard when it cannot be?
- **Who holds the reserve of ammunition** for the decisive fight, since movement battles consume fires at unpredictable rates?

The trainer's standard: fires and movement are rehearsed as one system. A unit that can bound but cannot call for suppression has learned half a maneuver, and the enemy will finish teaching it the other half at the worst possible tempo.

## 4.8 Trainer Objectives for This Chapter

- Pair each of the five schemes to a scenario where it is the correct main effort.
- Design a fixing force for a given flank attack: size, position, task, and exit criterion.
- Construct one deception event (feint or demonstration) that confirms a known enemy belief.
- Write a pursuit plan with direct and parallel forces and their logistic deadlines.
- Build the four-question fires plan for a movement force expecting near contact.
