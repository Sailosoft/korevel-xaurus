# Chapter 7 — Decision Cycles and Command Under Friction

**Series:** Strategic Advance & Tactical Operations Trainer (Chapter 7 of 10)

---

## 7.1 The Tempo Game

Every commander runs a loop: **observe, orient, decide, act.** The enemy's loop runs in parallel. You gain advantage not by being fast everywhere, but by completing your loop before the enemy completes theirs — forcing them to react to a situation that has already changed.

Operational movement is the physical expression of this race. Each bound, each flank, each feint is designed to invalidate the enemy's current orientation. The critical stage is **orient**, not act: a movement that changes how the enemy interprets the battlefield forces them to rebuild their decision from scratch, while a movement that merely changes positions invites a response they have already rehearsed.

## 7.2 Orders Architecture

Movement orders must survive transmission failure. The standard architecture:

1. **Situation** — enemy and friendly, stated as effects on movement.
2. **Mission** — one sentence: who, what, where, when, why.
3. **Execution** — commander's intent, main effort, tasks by maneuver, and coordinating instructions.
4. **Sustainment** — logistics and medical, computed to the limit.
5. **Command and signal** — who decides, where, and how the force talks.

The order is complete when a subordinate could execute it with no further guidance and would not be punished for improvising within the intent. If any element requires the commander's presence to be understood, the order is not an order — it is a script, and scripts fail at first contact.

## 7.3 Command by Mission, Control by Intent

In movement, detailed orders decay the moment contact begins. Command using:

- **Commander's intent** — the one paragraph that survives radio failure: what the force must achieve and why.
- **Mission tasks** — what each element must do, stated by effect, not by procedure.
- **Decision points** — pre-briefed conditions that trigger pre-briefed branches: "If bridge is down, then route Bravo, halt criteria X, report by Y."

The trained leader acts on the intent when the plan breaks; the untrained leader waits for orders and is overrun while waiting. Supervision during movement follows the same logic: the commander checks **conditions** at phase lines — spacing, security posture, schedule, readiness — rather than micromanaging positions on a map. Where the commander physically rides is itself a command decision; moving forward to see is correct, moving forward to control is not.

## 7.4 Friction and Its Management

Friction in movement is physical (mud, fuel, fatigue), informational (lost contact, wrong maps), and psychological (fear, boredom, overconfidence). Manage it with:

- **Checklists at phase lines,** not in the planning room. What must be true at each line of advance?
- **Simplicity audits:** every movement order should lose nothing if half the radios fail and one leader is down.
- **Battle rhythm:** scheduled reports and synchronized watches prevent the drift that turns a column into a straggling line.
- **Rest and rotation discipline:** fatigue causes most non-combat movement losses; schedule sleep like fuel.

The psychological layer deserves its own plan. Boredom degrades security faster than danger does — most ambushes succeed against forces that had been moving safely for hours. Overconfidence after easy success produces the halts, the skipped recon, and the silent radios that turn a second-class enemy into a first-class threat. Leaders counter both by injecting deliberate standards: recon that is actually tasked, drills that are actually run, intervals that are actually checked.

## 7.5 Halts, Bypasses, and Abort Criteria

- **Halt** only with a reason, a duration, and a security plan. A unit that halts without orders has already lost tempo.
- **Bypass** is the default against weak points that are not decisive; destruction of everything encountered is a strategy of exhaustion.
- **Abort criteria** must be written before movement: what losses, time limits, or intelligence turn the advance into a withdrawal? Leaders who decide to abort under fire are deciding too late.

Bypass decisions belong to the lowest leader who can make them safely and must be reported immediately: an unreported bypass turns the following element into the one that fights the fight you chose to skip.

## 7.6 Succession and Degraded Command

A movement plan must assume the command path will be cut. Build the system to keep deciding without its head:

- **Explicit succession:** every element names its first and second replacement before departure, and the replacements know their position in the column — a commander killed at the point is replaced by the leader at the point, not by whoever radios loudest from the rear.
- **Split command:** forward element for decisions, main headquarters for sustainment and coordination. The forward element carries the authority to decide; the main element carries the capacity to keep the force alive. Confusing the two produces a headquarters that fights like a platoon and a platoon that files paperwork.
- **Dead-reckoning orders:** when contact is lost, elements continue on the last order plus the standing rule — "on loss of higher: proceed to Phase Line Blue, hold, report at first light." Lost units that keep moving on the intent are reinforcing; lost units that wait are abandoned.

## 7.7 Reports That Command Can Use

Information flow decides whose loop is faster. The report standard for movement is fixed: **what happened, where on the ground, at what time, what it changes for the plan.** Reports that omit the "so what" force the commander to re-orient from raw data while the enemy already acts. Compress the cycle by pre-formatting contact reports, status reports, and recon answers — formats the unit knows by heart, so a stressed leader fills blanks instead of composing prose. The commander, in turn, answers every report that matters with a decision or a confirmation; unanswered reports teach the force that reporting is a tax, and the information flow dies with the lesson.

## 7.8 Trainer Objectives for This Chapter

- Write commander's intent for a movement mission that a section leader could execute with no further orders.
- Build a decision-point matrix with branches and sequels for a river-crossing approach.
- Conduct a simplicity audit on a live movement order under two simulated comms failures.
- Run a succession drill: leadership passes to the second replacement mid-movement without halting.
- Run a movement problem with injected comms failure and leader casualties; grade on intent-conformity, not plan-conformity.
