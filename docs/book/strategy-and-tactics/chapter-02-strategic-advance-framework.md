# Chapter 2 — The Strategic Advance Framework

**Series:** Strategic Advance & Tactical Operations Trainer (Chapter 2 of 10)

---

## 2.1 The Advance as a Campaign Shape

Strategic advance is the deliberate projection of force toward a decisive point. It is designed backward from the end state:

1. Define the **decisive objective** — the single condition whose achievement makes the remaining resistance irrelevant.
2. Identify the **lines of advance** that converge on it.
3. Sequence movements so each arrival sets conditions for the next.
4. Reserve the means to exploit success and to recover from failure.

The advance is a shape, not a line. A force that moves straight at the enemy's strongest position has a direction but no framework; a force whose movements converge, mislead, and hold depth has a framework even when individual actions fail. The planner's job is to make the enemy's defeat a consequence of geometry rather than attrition: every bound should either improve relative position or consume an enemy reserve that cannot be replaced.

Multi-axis advances follow three rules. **Converge** on the decisive objective so that separated forces reunite in time to fight together. **Diverge** in support of deception and area security, but never diverge the main effort. **Keep intervals covered** — the gap between axes is where counterattacks are born, and every gap requires either a screening force or a plan to collapse it on warning.

## 2.2 The Four Phases of Advance

**Phase I — Assembly and Shaping.** Concentrate forces, stage supplies, and shape enemy expectations. Movement in this phase is hidden or explained away. Success metric: readiness to cross the line of departure without further concentration.

**Phase II — Penetration or Displacement.** Cross into contested space. This is the highest-risk phase: the force transitions from posture to motion while the enemy reacts. Success metric: the lead elements establish a foothold that follow-on forces can pass through.

**Phase III — Exploitation.** Convert a local breach into operational freedom. Tempo dominates: the force must move faster than the enemy can reconstitute a defense. Success metric: enemy decision cycles consistently lag behind your movement.

**Phase IV — Consolidation.** Secure what was gained, reorganize, regenerate combat power, and prepare the next advance. Success metric: the force can defend what it took while still holding reserves for the next bound.

Phases do not end cleanly; they overlap. The transition from exploitation to consolidation is the classic defeat point, because the exploiting force is thinnest, furthest from supply, and most confident — the four ingredients of a counterattack's ideal target. The framework requires that consolidation be planned at the moment exploitation is ordered, not after it stops.

## 2.3 Bounds and Basing

Advanced movement proceeds in **bounds**: a force leaps from one provisional base to the next, always keeping enough elements stationary to cover the moving ones. Two bound patterns dominate:

- **Successive bounds:** units leapfrog along parallel routes; shorter distances, tighter control, slower tempo.
- **Alternating bounds:** one element moves while the other holds a phase line well to the front; longer distances, faster tempo, greater risk to the advancing unit.

The trainer's rule: choose alternating bounds when enemy contact is unlikely or the enemy is disintegrating; choose successive bounds when contact is probable and fire support must remain continuous.

Provisional bases must satisfy four checks: cover from observation, defensible ground, passable exits in at least two directions, and reach of the next bound's covering force. A bound that cannot be supported by fire from the base behind it is not a bound — it is an exposed march.

## 2.4 Decisive Point Selection

A decisive point is a location, asset, or function whose capture or neutralization disproportionately degrades enemy cohesion. Screen candidates with:

- **Effect** — how much enemy freedom of action is removed?
- **Reach** — can the force take it before the enemy reinforces it?
- **Sustain** — can the force hold it and continue from it?
- **Relevance** — does it serve the end state, or only the battle?

## 2.5 The Reserve Function

Every advance plan carries an uncommitted reserve until the decisive point is reached. The reserve exists for three purposes: to win the fight that the main effort cannot, to recover from the failure the plan assumed, and to signal to subordinate leaders that the commander still holds decision-making capacity. A force fully committed to its own plan has delegated its future to the enemy's behavior. Reserve sizing follows the risk gradient: larger during penetration, smallest during exploitation — and exploitation must then buy its own security with tempo, because the reserve has usually been spent or left behind.

## 2.6 Risk and Recovery

The framework requires explicit recovery plans for three failure modes: **stall** (the advance loses tempo before the objective), **repulse** (the penetration is thrown back), and **collapse** (a flank is turned). For each, the plan names the trigger condition, the responsible unit, the fallback position line, and the decision authority. Recovery pre-planned is a maneuver; recovery improvised under fire is a rout.

## 2.7 Sequencing and Synchronization

A four-phase advance becomes executable only when its movements are sequenced in time, not just in logic. The planning tools are fixed:

- **Movement tables:** time of departure, rate, halts, and passage points for every element, computed from the slowest required arrival, not the fastest possible march.
- **Priority of passage:** when two elements need the same bridge, defile, or landing zone, the plan already knows who wins — decided by the main effort, never by whoever shouts first.
- **Trigger events versus clock times:** some movements are scheduled by the clock; others start when a condition occurs ("on seizure of Phase Line Green, follow-on forces pass at 15-minute intervals"). Confusing the two is the most common cause of fratricide and congestion in combined advances.
- **Regeneration windows:** every bound consumes combat power; the schedule must contain named periods in which lead elements rest, resupply, and reorganize while the effort shifts to another element. An advance without regeneration is a retreat that has not started yet.

Synchronization is the operational commander's main technical burden: making separate movements arrive as one effect. The standard is that no element should ever be required to wait under observation for a force that is late — lateness is planned for, in the form of reserves of time, just as failure is planned for in the form of reserves of force.

## 2.8 Trainer Objectives for This Chapter

- Build a four-phase advance plan for an assigned objective, with phase-transition criteria.
- Select and defend a bound pattern for three terrain cases: open, confined, and permissive.
- Apply the decisive-point screen to competing candidate objectives.
- Write stall, repulse, and collapse recovery plans for a given advance.
- Produce a movement table with priority of passage and regeneration windows for a two-axis advance.
