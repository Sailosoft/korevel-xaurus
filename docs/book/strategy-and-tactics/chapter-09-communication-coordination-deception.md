# Chapter 9 — Communication, Coordination, and Deception

**Series:** Strategic Advance & Tactical Operations Trainer (Chapter 9 of 10)

---

## 9.1 Communication as the Spine of Movement

A moving force that cannot communicate is a collection of lost units. Design the network before the route:

- **Primary, alternate, contingency, emergency (PACE)** plans for every link: commander to advance guard, head to trail, element to overwatch.
- **Repeater and relay discipline:** terrain blocks line of sight; movement changes geometry constantly. Assign nodes to fix gaps, not hope.
- **Signals and brevity:** movement commands must survive radio silence, noise, and stress. Standardize halt, bound, breach, displace, and rally on single-word triggers.
- **Confirmation culture:** critical instructions are echoed, not acknowledged. "Pass at 0640" confirmed as "Pass at 0640, out" prevents two convoys sharing one bridge.

The network must also fail safely: every element knows what to do when it cannot reach the commander — continue on the last order, halt at the next phase line, or displace to the pre-briefed frequency. Silence with a rule is command; silence without a rule is abandonment.

## 9.2 Coordination Between Moving Elements

Synchronization is hardest exactly when the enemy is most vulnerable — during bounds and handovers:

- **Phase lines and trigger lines:** named geographic features that synchronize elements without radio traffic.
- **Handover standards:** when overwatch passes security responsibility to the next element, the pass is explicit, acknowledged, and rehearsed. Uncoordinated handovers create the gaps that ambushes exploit.
- **Fire control during movement:** the moment a lead element fires, the risk is not the enemy — it is your own supporting fires. Boundaries, no-fire areas, and weapon-safe arcs are movement essentials.
- **Liaison with adjacent forces:** your flank is someone else's front. Agree on recognition, routes, and timing before movement, never during.

Recognition signals — visual, infrared, electronic, and challenge-password — are coordination tools first and security tools second. A force that cannot identify its own elements at 200 meters will either hesitate at the wrong moment or shoot at the right one.

## 9.3 Deception in Operational Movement

Movement is the most deception-rich activity in warfare because it is the one the enemy must guess at rather than see:

- **False trails:** traffic patterns, dummy staging, and radio signatures on the wrong axis.
- **True concealment:** move the main body at night, on bad ground, in small bounds; let the enemy's attention follow the cheap show.
- **Timing deception:** one standard movement rhythm builds predictability; vary rhythm deliberately and keep the true pattern in the reserve of the plan.
- **The deception rule:** one lie, consistently supported. Multiple deceptions dilute each other and expose all of them.

Deception succeeds when the enemy's own intelligence cycle does the work for you: they will see what they expect. The planner's task is to know what the enemy expects — from the template of their template in Chapter 3 — and then to feed them that picture while the true movement proceeds elsewhere.

## 9.4 Electronic Emission and Silence

Every radio, radar, drone, and beacon emits a location. Emission control (EMCON) states are declared per phase: **silent** for the decisive movement, **selective** for routine march, **free** only when speed of information outweighs the risk of being found. The force that transmits freely while advancing has traded surprise for convenience and will pay for it at the line of departure.

Silence is not the absence of communication; it is the substitution of pre-arranged communication — signals, phase lines, scheduled events, and runners. A unit trained only to talk on radios is a unit that stops thinking when ordered silent.

## 9.5 Electronic and Physical Signature

Every movement emits signatures: thermal, acoustic, electronic, logistical (trash, tracks, fuel demand). Signature management is part of route selection, not an afterthought. The force that moves quietly chooses routes the enemy has not bothered to watch.

The counter-signature duty is equal: task sensors to read the enemy's emissions and signatures, because the enemy's logistics traffic, communications patterns, and patrol routines reveal their decision cycle as plainly as their positions do.

## 9.6 Civil-Military and Joint Interference

Operational movement rarely occurs on an empty ground. The coordination burden includes actors outside the force:

- **Civilian traffic and population:** shared routes create both a security problem (the enemy blends into movement you cannot restrict) and a strategic problem (a convoy that blocks a hospital or a market loses the campaign's quiet argument). Plan route-sharing rules, time windows, and liaison with local authorities before movement, and brief every driver on the civilian encounter drill.
- **Adjacent agencies and partners:** airspace, sea lanes, rail corridors, and infrastructure are contested resources. Deconfliction is done by named liaison and agreed schedules, discovered only when two helicopters occupy the same landing zone.
- **Information exposure:** the adversary's eyes include every phone on the route. Movement security now includes counter-imaging: assume observation from civilians, deny easy targeting by dispersing and masking, and never confirm the enemy's guess with an undisciplined signature.

The rule that ties these together: interference is not noise to be endured; it is terrain to be analyzed in Chapter 3 and coordinated in this one.

## 9.7 Signals Discipline and Counterintelligence

Communication security is part of movement security:

- **Assume interception:** content, rhythm, and volume of traffic tell the enemy your plan even when the codes hold. A sudden increase in transmissions before a dawn attack is itself the order of battle.
- **Standardize the harmless:** routine traffic sounds identical in format at all times, so the anomaly of real intent does not stand out.
- **Authenticate critical traffic:** challenge words and authentication procedures prevent the enemy from issuing your halt order in your voice — a trick that has stopped more convoys than mines.
- **Hunt your own leaks:** after every movement problem, review what the OPFOR learned and how; the most dangerous intelligence failures are the ones your own force broadcast for convenience.

## 9.8 Trainer Objectives for This Chapter

- Build a PACE communication plan for a three-element convoy in terrain-degraded comms.
- Rehearse a phase-line handover drill to zero ambiguity.
- Run an EMCON transition drill: free to silent without halting movement or losing coordination.
- Design a single-lie deception plan that masks the true axis of advance, with its supporting indicators.
- Conduct a traffic-pattern review: identify what an intercept officer could deduce about your plan from your own transmissions.
