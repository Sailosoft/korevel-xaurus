# Chapter 5 — Movement Formations and Convoys

**Series:** Strategic Advance & Tactical Operations Trainer (Chapter 5 of 10)

---

## 5.1 Formation Selection Variables

A formation is a trade-off triangle: **security, speed, and control.** You can optimize two; the third degrades. Select formations by answering:

1. What is the likely distance to first contact?
2. What direction must the force deliver fire fastest?
3. How wide are the routes?
4. What is the command-and-control span?

Formation discipline is the physical expression of the answer. A unit that cannot explain why it is in wedge rather than column has not chosen a formation — it has inherited a habit.

## 5.2 Standard Formations

- **Column (file):** maximum control and speed on narrow routes; minimum flank security. Use in permissive terrain and darkness.
- **Line:** maximum forward fire; slow, hard to control, exposes flanks. Use only for the final assault push.
- **Wedge:** balanced forward and flank security; the default for contact-capable movement. Requires width and daylight or good night vision.
- **Vee / echelon:** for when one flank is known to be threatened or impassable; security is biased to the live side.
- **Diamond / box:** all-around security on open terrain with heavy weapons organic; the standard for long-haul movement across flat, exposed ground.

Dismounted equivalents follow the same logic: file for speed and control, line-of-file for trails, wedge for open approach, skirmisher line for the final close. The trainer's standard is that every operator can convert mounted formations to dismounted ones and back within one bound, because terrain alternates and formations must alternate with it.

## 5.3 Convoy Architecture

A convoy is a system of five elements:

1. **Advance party / route security** — moves ahead to clear, mark, and report.
2. **Head** — commander, guide vehicle, communications.
3. **Main body** — cargo or combat vehicles, spaced by terrain and threat (tighter in concealment, wider in danger areas).
4. **Trail** — recovery, medical, and close-security rear element.
5. **Overwatch** — displaced to high ground or parallel routes, never in the column.

Rules:

- Never commit the whole convoy to a single choke point. Halve it at bridges, defiles, and tunnels.
- Every vehicle knows: immediate action on ambush (return fire, move out — never stop in the kill zone), rally points, and the order of march.
- The commander rides where the problem is, not where the paperwork is.
- Movement commands are passed forward, never backward, and confirmed vehicle by vehicle.

Spacing doctrine: intervals are set by threat, not by habit. Mine and IED risk widens intervals to reduce sympathetic damage; ambush risk shortens them so suppressing fire can cross-target; traffic and surface quality set the floor. Every interval is a statement about what the commander expects to happen — and the enemy gets a vote.

## 5.4 Convoy Types and Composition

- **Tactical convoy:** combat power moving as a unit; the order of march is a fighting order, with guns forward, on the flanks, and rear.
- **Logistics convoy:** supplies moving under guard; protection is outsourced to escort and route clearance, and the plan's center of gravity is the load itself.
- **Mixed and administrative movement:** personnel, light vehicles, and cargo combined; the discipline problem is greater than the security problem, because most of the column has no reason to be alert.

Standard composition for a movement echelon of any type: guide, security, main body, recovery, medical, and communications. If any of the six is missing, the plan must name who substitutes and who is told.

## 5.5 Danger Area Drills

Standardize these until they are reflex:

- **Halt / bound / resume** on call signs, not explanations.
- **Break contact:** near ambush — assault through; far ambush — maneuver element suppresses while support element flanks.
- **IED/obstacle discovery:** freeze, do not touch, mark, report, bypass with coverage, never reverse along the same path under threat.
- **Casualty in movement:** the vehicle that takes the casualty does not stop unless ordered; recovery is a task for the trail element.
- **Halt discipline:** scheduled halts are security events — perimeter, observation, rest, maintenance, and water, with departure time known to every element before the engines stop.

## 5.6 Rates, Halts, and Rally Points

Movement speed is a planned number, not an aspiration. **Rate of march** is set by the slowest required element, the surface, and the load — and then defended: a column that sprints for the first hour arrives tired, broken, and straggling for the last three. Standard cadence for sustained ground movement is a short security halt at the first interval after departure (to catch breakdowns and stragglers while they are still cheap), then regular halts of fixed duration, with a maintenance halt built into every long leg.

**Halt types** carry different postures: **immediate halts** freeze the column in place for observation or danger; **security halts** establish a perimeter and observation while resting; **maintenance halts** service vehicles and loads; **rest halts** feed and sleep the force and require the longest security plan. Every halt order states type, duration, and departure trigger.

**Rally points** are pre-designated for every failure: a near rally for a straggler or disabled vehicle, a far rally for a broken-down convoy, and an alternate route junction for a blocked axis. A force without rally points scatters; a force with them regroups without orders. The standard is that every operator can point to their rally from any position on the route and state the time they are expected there.

## 5.7 Trainer Objectives for This Chapter

- Select and justify a formation for three route profiles: open desert, forest track, urban street.
- Build a 12-vehicle convoy order of march with overwatch plan and halving points.
- Compute vehicle intervals for three threat cases: IED, ambush, and traffic.
- Write a halt schedule with types, durations, and departure triggers for a 90-kilometer movement.
- Run danger-area drills until unit response time is under ten seconds from trigger to action.
