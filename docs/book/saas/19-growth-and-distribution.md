A great product with no distribution dies quietly. For a solo founder,
growth cannot depend on a sales team or an ad budget. It must come from
**repeatable loops** and **cheap distribution channels** that compound
while you build. This chapter is about finding the two or three channels
that work for your specific buyer and turning them into engines.

The key insight: growth is not a bag of tactics, it is a system. Loops
beat funnels because loops feed themselves.

## By the end of this chapter

You will understand the difference between funnels and loops, how to
choose distribution channels that fit your buyer, how to build virality
and content engines for B2B, and how to measure what actually
compounds.

## Funnels versus loops

A funnel is linear: awareness, interest, decision, action. A loop is
circular: a customer's use of the product brings in the next customer.
Loops compound; funnels decay as costs rise.

| Loop type | How it works | B2B example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Viral | Users invite users | Team invites teammates |
| Content | Users create public artifacts | Shared reports with branding |
| Integration | Distribution through others' tools | App marketplace listing |
| Data | Usage improves the product | Benchmarks from aggregated data |
| Community | Users help and recruit users | Power-user forum or templates |

For a solo founder, loops that ride the product itself - viral invites
and content artifacts - are the most valuable, because they cost no
advertising and scale without headcount.

:::tip

Look for the loop hidden in your product. If your customers naturally
work with **other companies that look like them**, design the invite,
sharing, or export flow to reach those companies. The best growth
feature is often a small addition to an existing workflow, not a new
marketing channel.

:::

## Choose channels that fit your buyer

Do not chase every channel. Pick channels where your target buyer
already spends attention, then go deep.

| Channel | Best for | Solo-friendly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Content and SEO | Problem-aware buyers searching | Yes, compounds slowly |
| Communities | Niche, concentrated buyers | Yes |
| Cold email | Defined, reachable titles | Yes, needs care |
| Product-led sharing | Products with shared artifacts | Yes |
| Integrations/marketplaces | Buyers living in other tools | Yes |
| Paid ads | Validated funnel, budget | Rarely, expensive |
| Partnerships | Adjacent tools, agencies | Sometimes |

For most B2B solo founders, **content plus communities plus a
product-led loop** is the highest-return combination. It is slow at
first and compounding later, which is exactly the profile you want.

## Build the content engine

Content is distribution that keeps working after you publish. Do it
with a system, not bursts of inspiration.

- **Target questions, not topics.** Write for the searches your buyers
  make when they feel the pain (chapter 2).
- **One pillar, many pieces.** A definitive guide plus shorter posts,
  videos, and docs that link to it.
- **Show the outcome.** Tutorials and comparisons outperform vague
  thought leadership.
- **Repurpose ruthlessly.** One recording becomes a post, a doc, a
  template, and a thread.
- **Update, do not just add.** Refreshing old pieces often beats
  writing new ones.

| Content type | Intent | Compounds by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comparison pages | Buyer deciding | Search traffic for years |
| Tutorials | User trying to act | Reuse and links |
| Templates | Doer needing a start | Sharing and backlinks |
| Case studies | Skeptic seeking proof | Sales enablement |
| Changelog | Existing users | Trust and retention |

## Engineer virality for B2B

B2B virality is gentler than consumer virality, but it exists. The
invite-a-teammate loop from chapters 5 and 8 is the classic. Strengthen
it:

- **Make collaboration valuable**, so inviting a teammate genuinely
  helps the user.
- **Reduce friction** in the invite flow: fewer fields, clear value,
  one action.
- **Brand shared artifacts** - exported reports, public dashboards -
  so other companies discover you.
- **Time the ask** after the first value moment, never before.
- **Reward the inviter** with something meaningful, like extra seats or
  a discount.

Measure the loop honestly: track **invites sent, accepted, and
activated per account**. A loop that sends many invites but activates
few is not a loop, it is noise.

:::caution

Never fake virality with gimmicks like "invite three friends to
unlock." B2B buyers see through it, and it damages trust with the
exact audience you need. Real loops make the product better for the
inviter, not worse for the invitee.

:::

## Measure what compounds

Growth metrics should focus on loops and retention, not vanity totals.

| Metric | Definition | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CAC | Cost to acquire a customer | Channel efficiency |
| Payback period | Months to recover CAC | Cash health |
| Viral coefficient | Invites that become customers | Loop strength |
| Organic share | Traffic/signups from free channels | Compounding |
| Channel ROI | Revenue per channel spend | Where to invest |

Track CAC against **LTV** (lifetime value) and payback. A channel that
looks cheap but brings customers who churn in two months is expensive.

## The growth checklist

- [ ] Identify one loop native to your product.
- [ ] Pick two or three channels that match your buyer.
- [ ] Build a content engine targeting real buyer questions.
- [ ] Strengthen the invite and sharing flows.
- [ ] Brand shared artifacts for discovery.
- [ ] Time every growth ask after the first value moment.
- [ ] Measure CAC, payback, viral coefficient, and organic share.
- [ ] Double down on the channel that compounds; cut the rest.

## Key takeaways

- **Loops beat funnels**; design growth that feeds itself.
- Choose channels where your buyer already spends attention.
- **Content compounds**: target questions, repurpose, and refresh.
- Engineer B2B virality through collaboration and **branded shared
  artifacts**.
- Avoid gimmicks; make invites valuable for the inviter.
- Measure **CAC against LTV and payback**, not raw totals.

Growth brings customers in. The final chapter ties it all together
with the scorecard that tells you if the business itself is healthy.
