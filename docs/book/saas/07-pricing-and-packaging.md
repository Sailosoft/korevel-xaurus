title: Pricing, Packaging, and Plans
description: Chapter 7 - how a solo founder prices a B2B SaaS, packages plans, and raises prices without losing customers.
---

Pricing is the fastest lever in SaaS. A ten percent price increase flows
almost entirely to profit, while a ten percent acquisition improvement
costs far more effort to achieve. Yet founders agonize over features and
guess at prices. This chapter gives you a pricing method you can defend
and revise.

The core mistake is pricing on cost or on competitor tables. Price on
**value delivered to a specific buyer**, then package plans so each
customer can choose the tier that matches their willingness to pay.

## By the end of this chapter

You will know how to pick a pricing metric, construct three tiers, set
anchors, test price changes safely, and handle the inevitable moment
when you need to raise prices on existing customers.

## Choose your pricing metric

A pricing metric is the unit your price scales with. It should correlate
with the value the customer gets - if it does, growing customers
naturally pay more without resentment.

| Metric | Good when | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Per seat | Value grows with team | Discourages adoption, seat sharing |
| Per usage | Value is consumption of the core | Unpredictable bills scare buyers |
| Per outcome | Value is a result (orders, invoices) | Hard to measure cleanly |
| Flat per tenant | Simple, single buyer, fixed scope | Leaves money on the table |
| Hybrid (base + usage) | Predictable floor, upside | Complexity for a solo founder |

For most solo founders, a **flat per-tenant base with a per-seat or
per-usage upper tier** is the pragmatic sweet spot: predictable enough
to sell, scalable enough to grow.

:::tip

If you are unsure, start with flat pricing for a single buyer and add a
usage-based tier only when a customer's value clearly outpaces their
bill. Overcomplicating pricing early is as costly as undercharging.

:::

## Build three tiers

Three tiers cover almost every B2B decision: a small entry point, a
main tier you want most customers to buy, and an anchor tier that makes
the middle look reasonable.

| Tier | Role | Typical shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starter | Entry, low friction | Core outcome, one seat, limits |
| Growth | The target | More seats, integrations, support |
| Scale | Anchor and enterprise door | SSO, audit logs, priority, SLA |

The middle tier is your default. Design the tiers so the middle is
obviously the best value for your target buyer, and let the top tier
exist partly to reframe it. Enterprise buyers who need SSO and audit
logs will climb upward anyway; you do not need a sales team to capture
them if the top tier says "contact us."

## Set price levels deliberately

Price against value, not feelings. A simple framework:

1. **Estimate the buyer's alternative cost.** What do they spend on the
   workaround - labor hours, other tools, errors?
2. **Price a fraction of that.** Capturing ten to thirty percent of
   demonstrable value is usually an easy yes.
3. **Anchor high on the page.** Show the top tier first or largest;
   anchoring makes the middle feel affordable.
4. **Round charm, not random.** $49 / $99 / $299 reads intentional;
   $47.33 reads desperate.

Use the value gap as your ceiling, and your costs as a floor - never as
the strategy. A customer saving $2,000 a month will happily pay $200.

## Package for expansion

Packaging is how you monetize growth without renegotiating every
account. Add expansion levers inside tiers:

- **Seats** that grow with the team.
- **Usage limits** that scale with volume.
- **Feature gates** that unlock at the next tier (integrations, SSO,
  audit logs).
- **Support levels** that escalate from email to priority.
- **Add-ons** for distinct value (extra storage, premium integrations).

The goal: a customer can grow from Starter to Scale **without a new
sales conversation**. Self-serve expansion is the solo founder's
superpower.

:::caution

Do not gate the core outcome to force upgrades. If Starter cannot
deliver the promised result at all, you have a bait-and-switch, not a
tier. Gate scale, support, and secondary features - never the primary
job the customer bought.

:::

## Test and change prices safely

Price changes should be experiments, not guesses:

- **New customers first.** Raise prices for new signups before touching
  existing accounts.
- **Hold the old price for a cohort.** Grandfathering builds goodwill
  and gives you a natural A/B comparison.
- **Watch churn, not just conversion.** A price that lifts revenue but
  triples churn is a loss.
- **Announce increases with value.** Give notice, explain the added
  value, and offer an annual lock-in at the old price.

When you do raise prices for existing customers, do it in writing, far
ahead, and with a grace period. Ten percent more revenue is not worth
ten percent more resentment.

## Discounts and annual plans

Discounts are a tool with sharp edges. Use them strategically:

- **Annual prepay:** offer roughly two months free to improve cash flow
  and reduce churn. This is usually your highest-value discount.
- **Startup or nonprofit programs:** goodwill and word of mouth, with
  clear eligibility.
- **Volume discounts:** only above a threshold where margins hold.
- **Avoid permanent discounts:** they reset the customer's price anchor
  and are hard to unwind.

Track discount rate as a metric. If it creeps up, your list prices may
be too high - or your sales motion too eager.

## Pricing checklist

- [ ] Pick a pricing metric that correlates with customer value.
- [ ] Define three tiers with a clear target tier in the middle.
- [ ] Anchor price to a fraction of the buyer's alternative cost.
- [ ] Provide self-serve expansion levers (seats, usage, features).
- [ ] Never gate the core outcome; gate scale and support instead.
- [ ] Test increases on new customers first, with cohort
      grandfathered.
- [ ] Offer annual prepay and track discount rate.
- [ ] Communicate any increase with notice and value framing.

## Key takeaways

- Price on **value to a specific buyer**, not on cost or competitors.
- Choose a **pricing metric** that scales with value delivered.
- Three tiers - entry, target, anchor - cover nearly every decision.
- **Package for self-serve expansion** so customers can grow without
  you.
- Test prices on **new customers first** and watch churn, not just
  conversion.
- Use **annual prepay** and disciplined discounts; avoid permanent
  price cuts.

actually reach that value - onboarding and activation.
