You have built the modules. Now you need the scoreboard. A SaaS business
lives or dies by a small set of numbers, and a solo founder must know
them cold - both to steer and to know when to raise, sell, or keep
compounding. This chapter is the scorecard, the triggers that change
your strategy, and the long game.

The final reframe: a SaaS is not finished when the modules ship. It is
finished when the numbers are healthy and the machine runs without
heroics.

## By the end of this chapter

You will know the core SaaS metrics and how they interact, the
benchmarks that indicate health, the strategic decisions that follow
from them - bootstrapping versus raising, building versus selling - and
how to run the long game so the business compounds.

## The core scorecard

Track a handful of metrics weekly and understand how they connect. Each
one tells a part of the story.

| Metric | Definition | What it reveals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MRR | Recurring revenue per month | Growth and size |
| ARR | MRR times twelve | Annual scale |
| New MRR | New customers added | Acquisition |
| Expansion MRR | Upgrades and add-ons | Growth within base |
| Churned MRR | Lost recurring revenue | Leakage |
| Net revenue retention | Cohort revenue over time | Net health |
| Logo churn | Customers lost / total | Stickiness |
| CAC | Cost to acquire a customer | Efficiency |
| LTV | Lifetime value per customer | Value created |
| Payback | Months to recover CAC | Cash efficiency |

The relationship that matters most: **net revenue retention above
100%** means existing customers spend more over time than they take
away, and growth compounds even if acquisition stalls.

## Benchmarks and what they mean

Use benchmarks as signals, not laws. Your segment and stage change the
targets.

| Metric | Weak | Healthy | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Net revenue retention | Under 90% | 100-110% | 120%+ |
| Logo churn (monthly, B2B SMB) | Over 5% | 2-3% | Under 1% |
| LTV:CAC | Under 3:1 | 3-5:1 | 5:1+ |
| Payback period | Over 18 months | 12-18 months | Under 12 months |
| Gross margin | Under 60% | 70-85% | 85%+ |

These numbers interact. A high CAC is survivable with strong retention,
and a high churn rate makes even cheap acquisition expensive. Read them
together, never in isolation.

:::tip

Review the scorecard weekly on one page and the deeper cohorts
monthly. Weekly keeps you responsive; monthly keeps you honest about
trends. A founder who checks metrics daily panics; one who checks
yearly drifts. Weekly is the sweet spot.

:::

## Diagnostics: what to do when numbers move

Metrics are only useful if they change your behavior. A simple
diagnostic map:

- **High churn:** go to chapters 8 (activation) and 18 (success) - the
  problem is usually onboarding or value delivery, not price.
- **Low net revenue retention:** chapter 7 (packaging) - you may be
  missing expansion levers.
- **High CAC:** chapter 19 (channels) - the channel or the targeting is
  wrong.
- **Weak activation:** chapter 8 - shorten time to value.
- **Flat growth with good retention:** chapter 19 - distribution, not
  product, is the constraint.
- **Rising support volume:** chapter 18 and the product - recurring
  tickets are a build signal.

The scorecard tells you **which chapter** of this book to revisit. That
is its real value: it points at the binding constraint.

## Strategic forks

Once the numbers are stable, strategy is a series of deliberate forks.

**Bootstrapping versus raising.** Bootstrapping keeps ownership and
control; it caps speed. Raising buys speed; it costs equity and adds
responsibility. For a narrow, efficient B2B product with healthy
retention, bootstrapping often wins. Raise when capital turns a
proven loop into a much bigger one.

**Product-led versus sales-led.** Product-led grows through self-serve
(chapters 8 and 19). Sales-led adds human motion for larger deals. Most
solo founders start product-led and add light sales motion only for
enterprise accounts.

**Vertical depth versus horizontal breadth.** Depth serves one industry
extremely well; breadth serves many adequately. Depth usually wins for
solo founders because focus compounds and word of mouth concentrates.

**Build versus buy (again).** As you scale, revisit the buy-or-build
decision from chapter 1. Once a module becomes a differentiator, own
it; otherwise keep buying and stay focused on the outcome.

:::caution

The most common strategic mistake late in the journey is **undisciplined
expansion** - adding a second customer type, a second product, or a new
market before the first is durable. It feels like growth; it is
dilution. Only expand when the core loop is proven and the numbers say
you can afford the focus cost.

:::

## The long game

SaaS rewards patience more than any other software model. The mechanics:

- **Retention compounds.** Every month a customer stays, they are worth
  more - and refer more.
- **Trust compounds.** Reliability, support, and security build a moat
  competitors cannot copy quickly.
- **Content compounds.** Docs, guides, and reputation earn traffic for
  years.
- **Focus compounds.** One product, one buyer, one outcome, repeated
  for a decade, beats ten pivots.

The exit is optional. A healthy, profitable, focused SaaS with strong
net revenue retention is a durable asset whether or not you ever sell
it. Acquisition is a liquidity event, not the goal. The goal is a
machine that reliably turns a narrow outcome into recurring revenue you
own.

## The final checklist

- [ ] Track MRR, NRR, logo churn, CAC, LTV, and payback weekly.
- [ ] Review cohorts monthly and set realistic benchmarks.
- [ ] Use the diagnostic map to find the binding constraint.
- [ ] Decide bootstrapping versus raising deliberately.
- [ ] Choose product-led or sales-led based on your buyer.
- [ ] Commit to one vertical until the loop is proven.
- [ ] Revisit buy-or-build as modules mature.
- [ ] Run the long game: retention, trust, content, and focus.

## Key takeaways

- A SaaS lives or dies by a **small set of connected metrics**; know
  them cold.
- **Net revenue retention above 100%** is the signal that growth
  compounds.
- Use the scorecard to identify **which constraint to fix**, and
  revisit the relevant chapter.
- Make strategic forks - bootstrap or raise, product-led or sales-led,
  depth or breadth - **deliberately**.
- Avoid undisciplined expansion; **focus is the durable advantage**.
- Run the long game: **retention, trust, content, and focus compound**
  into a business you own.

The modules are complete, the scorecard is in place. From here, the
work is rhythm: ship weekly, measure weekly, talk to customers, keep
the loops turning. That cadence - not any single feature - is what
makes a solo B2B SaaS endure.
